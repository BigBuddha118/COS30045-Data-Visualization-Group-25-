About the data

Where the figures come from, how they were prepared, and what to keep in mind when reading the charts.

Purpose

This site explains Australian state and territory police road enforcement in plain language: how many drivers are tested for alcohol and drugs, and how many fines are issued. It is built with HTML, CSS and D3.js, and every chart is drawn in the browser from the data files in the data folder.

Data source

The figures are police enforcement counts for Australia’s eight states and territories, 2008 to 2024, supplied as four Excel workbooks (police_enforcement_2024_alcohol_drug_tests, _fines, _positive_breath_tests and _positive_drug_tests) and two summary CSV files (enforcement_state_year.csv and fines_state_year_offence.csv).

Hospitalised road-crash injuries (2011–2021)

The Hospitalisations page uses three additional source workbooks: hospitalisation_injury_publication_sep2023.xlsx, state-and-territory-hospitalisation-injury-publication-2023.xlsx, and First-Nations-hospitalised-injuries-2023.xlsx. Their published Table 1 and Table 2 series are exported to four browser-friendly CSV files. The hospitalisation series ends in 2021, unlike the police enforcement series (2024).

Hospitalisations count injury admissions/cases rather than road crashes or unique drivers. State totals and population subgroups should not be directly compared as risk rates without appropriate denominators and reporting context. First Nations and non-Indigenous values do not sum to the overall total because status may be unknown or otherwise excluded.

How the data was prepared

  • The two summary CSVs were tidied: columns renamed, blank cells kept as missing rather than turned into zero, and Sum checked against the workbooks and confirmed to be the number of charges.
  • Only fines are charted. Arrests and charges are left out because some jurisdictions (Queensland and Victoria) report none, which would look like a real zero.
  • The drug-type chart uses the positive drug test workbook for 2024, keeping each test once (at its best detection stage), and only the five jurisdictions that record the drug found. The total matches the summary CSV exactly.
  • The age chart uses the 2024 fines workbook and leaves out fines where the age is unknown.

Missing and incomplete data

  • Tasmania does not report positive breath tests for 2020–2024.
  • The Northern Territory has drug test totals for only two years (2021 and 2022), the ACT is missing 2008–2011 and Tasmania is missing 2009. Drug test rates are therefore not charted.
  • Speeding fines are missing for one jurisdiction in 2008, 2009 and 2022, seatbelt fines start in 2017 and cover all eight jurisdictions only from 2020, and unlicensed driving fines exist only for 2023 and 2024. The long-run trend is therefore shown for mobile phone fines, which all eight report for every year.
  • Some figures are a count of tests or fines, not of people: one driver can be tested or fined more than once.

Privacy and ethics

The data is aggregated: totals by state, year, age group and offence. It contains no names or other personal details, and nobody can be identified from it.

Enforcement data describes police activity, not the behaviour of the public. A state that issues more fines, or finds a higher share of positive tests, is not necessarily a state with worse drivers. For that reason the charts avoid ranking states against each other and each one is followed by a note on what it cannot show.

Common questions

What counts as a positive test?

A positive breath test is one over the legal limit. A positive drug test is one where a drug was detected.

Why aren’t states ranked against each other?

States test in different ways. Random testing of very large numbers of drivers tends to produce a low share of positives, while more targeted testing tends to produce a higher share, so the shares can’t be compared fairly. The data does not record how drivers were chosen.

What is the difference between a fine and a charge?

A fine is a penalty issued on the spot. A charge starts a court process and is used for more serious offences. Some offences, such as unlicensed driving, lead to charges more often than fines.

Why do some charts have gaps?

Where a state did not report a number, it is left blank and the chart skips it, rather than showing it as zero.
