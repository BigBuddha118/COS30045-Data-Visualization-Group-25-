Alcohol and drug testing

Police test drivers for alcohol with breath tests and for drugs with roadside drug tests. These charts show how much testing happens, how often it finds a positive result, and which drugs are found.

How many drivers are breath tested?

The chart shows breath tests conducted each year from 2008 to 2024. Use the buttons to switch between all states combined and each state or territory, and hover over a dot to read the value.

Show:
What this shows National breath testing ran at roughly 13 to 16 million tests a year until 2019. In 2020 it fell to 7.3 million — 52% lower — and all 8 states and territories tested fewer drivers than in 2019. By 2024 the total was 10.3 million, still 33% below 2019.
Read with care The timing matches the COVID-19 period, but this data records how many tests took place, not why the number changed.

Why do some states find more positive tests?

A positive breath test means the driver’s reading was over the legal limit. Each dot below is one state or territory in one year (131 dots in total; Tasmania’s positive results are not reported for 2020–2024). The further right a dot is, the more drivers were tested; the higher it is, the larger the share of tests that were positive. Use the buttons to pick out one state.

Highlight:
What this shows Jurisdictions that test more drivers tend to find a smaller share positive (a rank correlation of -0.74 across the 131 points). In 2024 the Northern Territory recorded 8.2% positive tests, while every other jurisdiction with data was between 0.27% and 0.82%.
Read with care A higher share does not mean drivers there drink-drive more. One likely explanation is that when police test very large numbers of drivers at random, most tests are negative, while more targeted testing finds a higher share. The data does not record how drivers were chosen, so compare a state with its own past rather than with other states.

Which drugs show up in roadside drug tests?

In 2024, 55,391 positive drug tests were recorded across the eight jurisdictions. Only the ACT, New South Wales, South Australia, Victoria and Western Australia record which drug was found. Those five account for 37,561 positive tests (68%), so the chart covers them.

What this shows Amphetamines (including methylamphetamine) were involved in 61% of these positive tests and cannabis in 46%, far ahead of cocaine (7.5%) and ecstasy (3.6%). The percentages add up to more than 100% because one test can detect more than one drug.
Read with care States define their drug categories and testing stages slightly differently — South Australia, for example, records methylamphetamine separately, so it is combined with amphetamines here. Each test is counted once, at its best available detection stage. These are counts of positive tests, not of drivers and not a measure of impairment.
