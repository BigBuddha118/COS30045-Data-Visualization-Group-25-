Fines

Police issue fines for offences such as speeding, using a mobile phone, not wearing a seatbelt and driving unlicensed. These charts show which offences lead to the most fines, how mobile phone fines have changed, and who receives them.

Which offences lead to the most fines?

The donut adds up fines issued in 2024 for four offences across all eight jurisdictions. Hover over a slice or a legend entry for the detail.

What this shows Speeding accounted for 85.5% of the 3,888,724 fines (3,323,333). Mobile phone use made up 8.5%, seatbelts 4.7% and unlicensed driving 1.3%.
Read with care Fines are only part of enforcement. Unlicensed driving is mostly dealt with by charges rather than fines: there were 56,502 charges against 51,150 fines in 2024, even though QLD and VIC show no charges at all. So its small slice understates how often it is policed. Other offences, such as drink-driving, are not part of this chart.

How have mobile phone fines changed?

Each panel shows one state or territory’s mobile phone fines per year from 2008 to 2024. Every panel has its own scale, because the numbers differ enormously: New South Wales issued 161,997 fines in 2024, the Northern Territory just 244. Hover over a panel to read the value for a year.

What this shows Three jurisdictions show large jumps. New South Wales went from 25,463 fines in 2019 to 151,725 in 2020 and 216,042 in 2023. Queensland went from 4,823 in 2020 to 121,639 in 2022. The ACT went from 288 in 2023 to 27,125 in 2024.
Read with care These jumps line up with camera enforcement: in 2024, cameras detected about 91% of New South Wales, 96% of Queensland and 99% of ACT mobile phone fines (Victoria: 77%). The data shows how fines were detected, but not when each state introduced cameras, or whether more drivers started using phones. A rise in fines should not be read as a rise in phone use.

Who receives the fines?

This chart shows 2024 fines by the age group of the driver. Use the buttons to look at one offence or all four together.

Offence:
What this shows Across all four offences, drivers aged 40–64 received the most fines (about 1.24 million), followed by those aged 26–39 (about 0.90 million). Drivers aged 65 and over received about 330 thousand and drivers under 17 about 23 thousand.
Read with care These are counts, not rates. The age groups cover different numbers of years (40–64 spans 25 years, 17–25 spans 9) and contain different numbers of drivers, so a taller bar does not mean those drivers offend more. Age is only recorded for 52% of mobile phone fines, 54% of seatbelt fines, 78% of speeding fines and 87% of unlicensed driving fines; fines without a known age are left out.
